\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_motivation}

Gait authentication offers a practically appealing form of continuous biometric verification: a smartphone already carries the accelerometers and gyroscopes needed to recognise a user from the way they walk, without requiring any deliberate interaction. The signal is passive, continuous, and does not depend on hardware beyond the device the user already carries.

The system evaluated in this work separates gait authentication into two components with different roles. A CNN encoder is trained to learn general-purpose gait representations from a population of subjects; its weights can then be distributed to any deployment. An LSTM authenticator is trained on top of those frozen representations for a specific operator's subject population, producing the pairwise accept or reject decision. This separation is operationally attractive but raises a specific privacy question: what does the trained system reveal about the individuals whose data it was built on?

Membership inference — determining whether a particular individual was in a model's training set~\cite{shokri2017membership} — is the formal version of that question. For a gait authentication model the question has concrete stakes: gait encodes information about age, health, and physical state, and confirming that a person's data was used to train a deployed authenticator has implications beyond the immediate system. The standard membership inference attack operates on a model's per-class confidence output, but an authentication model produces no such output: it scores pairs of windows, not individual samples. The membership signal, if one exists, must be derived from the structure of those pairwise score distributions — and it is not obvious that such a signal exists, nor how strong it is.

Following that signal raises a further question: where does it originate? The CNN encoder and the LSTM authenticator are trained on the same subjects in the standard configuration, so their individual contributions to any observed vulnerability are entangled. The answer matters for mitigation: a signal concentrated in the encoder calls for a different response than one concentrated in the authenticator.

The evasion attack asks a complementary question about the system's decision boundary. A small perturbation applied to an impostor's probe window via projected gradient descent may be sufficient to cross the acceptance threshold. How large that perturbation must be, relative to natural within-subject gait variability, characterises how close the boundary sits to naturally occurring gait. Unexpectedly, the per-subject evasion budget itself carries membership information: the boundary sits differently for subjects the model was trained on, connecting the robustness and inference problems in a way that was not anticipated at the outset.

The mitigation explored in the final chapter of this work did not begin as a privacy solution. The same two-component pipeline faces an operational problem when multiple operators train on the CNN encoder sequentially, each adding their own subjects without sharing data. A feature-map code mechanism, designed to isolate gradient updates across sequential training rounds and prevent catastrophic forgetting, requires each operator to condition the LSTM on a private key applied to the CNN outputs. That key conditions the authentication surface: a model trained with one key does not respond correctly to a different one. The operational constraint and the privacy requirement turn out to have the same solution. Chapter~\ref{ch:cl_mia} derives the threat model that makes this precise: why the key must be operator-level rather than subject-level, and why it must reside in the LSTM rather than the CNN.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

This work addresses two attack problems on a gait-based authentication system built on the CNN architecture from Zou et al.~\cite{zou2020deep}.

\textbf{Membership inference:} a trained authentication model may leak information about its training set: a subject whose gait was used during training tends to produce a systematically different score distribution from a held-out subject, and this asymmetry constitutes a membership signal. The extent of this vulnerability is not established for this architecture, and the origin of the signal within the pipeline is not known. Both the CNN encoder and the LSTM authentication head are trained on the same subjects in the standard configuration, so their individual contributions to the observed signal cannot be separated without a dedicated experimental design in which the two components are trained on non-overlapping subject populations.

\textbf{Evasion:} the authentication model's decision boundary may be vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations: a probe window modified by projected gradient descent may cross the acceptance threshold even though it originates from an impostor. The severity of this vulnerability depends on how large the required perturbation is relative to the natural trial-to-trial variability of the gait signal. Additionally, the perturbation budget required to breach the boundary for a given subject may itself carry membership information, linking the evasion problem back to the inference problem.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The main contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Membership inference evaluation across multiple datasets and threat models.}
  The membership inference vulnerability of the authentication system is characterised under several attack variants that differ in how much the attacker knows about the target model, from full access to the model weights down to a black-box setting. The evaluation spans four dataset configurations: three within-dataset runs and a cross-dataset run in which the encoder and authenticator are trained on different subject populations.

  \item \textbf{Memorisation signal disentanglement.}
  A dedicated experiment isolates how much of the observed membership signal originates from the CNN encoder and how much from the LSTM authentication head. Subjects are partitioned into non-overlapping groups so that the two components are trained on disjoint populations, allowing their individual contributions to the signal to be measured and compared.

  \item \textbf{Evasion attack evaluation.}
  The robustness of the authentication boundary is characterised through a PGD evasion attack across all four dataset configurations. Two complementary parameterisations are evaluated: one that searches for the minimum perturbation budget at a fixed number of gradient steps, and one that fixes the budget and searches for the minimum number of steps required for the attack to succeed. Both measures are compared against the natural intra-subject gait variability and used as secondary membership inference signals.

  \item \textbf{Feature-map code defence under continual learning.}
  A binary key applied at the CNN feature maps is shown to address the forgetting and privacy problems simultaneously: it isolates gradient updates across sequential enrolment rounds and conditions the authentication surface on a private key, blocking membership inference for operators who hold the model but not the key. Authentication performance and residual privacy risk are evaluated with and without the correct key.

\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro_outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} introduces the technical background: the properties of gait signals that make them suitable for authentication, the architecture of the CNN and LSTM components used in this work, and the formal framework for membership inference attacks including the LiRA construction.

Chapter~\ref{ch:related} surveys prior work on gait-based authentication, membership inference on biometric and machine learning systems, and adversarial attacks on authentication models, and positions this work relative to that literature.

Chapter~\ref{ch:system} describes the three datasets used in the evaluation, the subject split and pair construction procedure, the CNN encoder and authentication model architectures, and the four experimental configurations. This chapter establishes the shared experimental setup on which all subsequent chapters depend.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evasion} presents the evasion attack: the PGD formulation, the perturbation budget calibration against natural gait variability, and the attack results across all four dataset configurations. Two complementary parameterisations are evaluated, one searching over the perturbation budget and one searching over the number of gradient steps at a fixed budget. The per-subject attack statistics produced here feed directly into the membership inference analysis of the following chapter.

Chapter~\ref{ch:mia} presents the membership inference experiments. Several attack variants under different attacker knowledge assumptions are evaluated across four dataset configurations, including the evasion-derived signals from the previous chapter. The chapter closes with the signal disentanglement experiment, which uses non-overlapping subject groups to isolate the CNN encoder's and LSTM authenticator's independent contributions to the membership signal.

Chapter~\ref{ch:cl_mia} evaluates the feature-map code defence as a response to the vulnerabilities identified in the two preceding chapters. A task-specific binary key applied at the CNN feature maps is shown to serve as a continual learning mechanism and as a privacy protection simultaneously. Authentication accuracy and membership inference vulnerability are compared across different key configurations.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises the findings, discusses the limitations of the experimental design, and identifies directions for future work.
