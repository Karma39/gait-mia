\chapter{Evasion Attack}
\label{ch:evasion}
\input{../generated/nb06c_whuGAIT_metrics}

This chapter measures how close the authentication model's decision boundary is to naturally occurring gait signals. For each impostor pair the model correctly rejects, a perturbation is optimised via PGD to push the output below the acceptance threshold. The minimum budget required for success is calibrated against the natural within-subject variability of the signal, giving a reference scale for practical severity. Two complementary parameterisations are studied: one fixes the number of gradient steps and searches for the minimum budget $\varepsilon_\text{min}$; the other fixes the budget and searches for the minimum step count $N_\text{min}$. Both signals are later used as membership inference signals in Chapter~\ref{ch:mia}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Attack Formulation}
\label{sec:ev_formulation}

\subsection{False Accept Objective}
\label{ssec:ev_objective}

The attack targets impostor pairs: two windows from different subjects that the authentication model would correctly reject. The attacker controls the probe window $\mathbf{x}_1$ and adds a perturbation $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ before it reaches the model. The reference window $\mathbf{x}_2$ stored in the authentication database is untouched. The goal is not to mimic a specific subject's gait but to push the model's output score across the decision boundary.

The attack succeeds when the perturbed pair is accepted:
\begin{equation}
  P\!\left(\text{different person} \mid \mathbf{x}_1 + \boldsymbol{\delta},\,
  \mathbf{x}_2\right) < 0.5.
\end{equation}
The attack surface is the raw sensor signal: the 6-channel, 128-timestep IMU window. The CNN and LSTM are not modified; the gradient flows through both during optimisation.

\subsection{Norm Choice and Optimisation}
\label{ssec:ev_pgd}

The $\ell_2$ norm is chosen over $\ell_\infty$ for both physical and empirical reasons. A natural change in human gait distributes energy continuously across all sensor channels and all timesteps: a slightly longer stride or a shift in arm swing affects the full window, not a handful of isolated samples. An $\ell_\infty$ constraint allows the perturbation to concentrate its entire budget into a few large spikes at individual sensor readings, which correspond to sharp impact events that are physically implausible and easy to detect. An $\ell_2$ constraint instead bounds the total perturbation energy and encourages smooth, broadband changes that more closely resemble natural intra-subject variation. This choice also connects directly to the budget calibration: $\varepsilon_\text{target}$ is itself an $\ell_2$ distance (Section~\ref{sec:ev_budget}), making the budget ratio $\varepsilon_\text{min}/\varepsilon_\text{target}$ directly interpretable.

The perturbation is found by standard PGD~\cite{madry2018towards}: starting from the clean probe $\mathbf{x}_1$, each of $K$ steps takes a gradient step on the cross-entropy loss with target label 0 (same person) and projects the result back onto the $\ell_2$ ball of radius $\varepsilon$.

\section{Perturbation Budget Estimation}
\label{sec:ev_budget}

\subsection{Intra-Subject L2 as Reference Scale}
\label{ssec:ev_l2}

Rather than fixing $\varepsilon$ arbitrarily, the budget is anchored to the natural variability of the signal being attacked. For each dataset, $\varepsilon_\text{target}$ is defined as the median $\ell_2$ distance between two windows recorded from the same subject in the training split:
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_\text{target}
  = \mathrm{median}_{(i,j)\,:\,s_i = s_j}
    \left\| \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \right\|_2.
\end{equation}
A perturbation with $\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_2 \leq \varepsilon_\text{target}$ is smaller than the typical difference between two windows from the same person, giving a reference point for what the model should already tolerate as natural intra-subject variation.

The resulting values are $\varepsilon_\text{target} = \whuGAITEpsTarget$ (whuGAIT), $\uciharEpsTarget$ (UCI-HAR), $\wisdmEpsTarget$ (WISDM), and $\combinedEpsTarget$ (combined). The differences reflect window length and sampling rate: WISDM's 20\,Hz, 128-sample windows span 6.4 seconds and accumulate more cycle-to-cycle variation than shorter windows. Because $\varepsilon_\text{target}$ is estimated from training-split (member) windows only, it may not perfectly represent the natural variability of held-out subjects; the budget ratio $\varepsilon_\text{min}/\varepsilon_\text{target}$ should be interpreted as a reference scale rather than a strict normalisation.

\section{Two Attack Parameterisations}
\label{sec:ev_parameterisations}

\subsection{Fixed-Step Budget Search}
\label{ssec:ev_eps_param}

The first parameterisation fixes the number of gradient steps at $K = 20$ and searches for the minimum budget $\varepsilon_\text{min}$. For each impostor pair $(A, B)$, $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ is found in two phases. First, a geometric grid of $\varepsilon$ values spanning $[5\%\,\varepsilon_\text{target},\, \varepsilon_\text{target}]$ plus sparse sentinels at $2\times$, $5\times$, and $10\times \varepsilon_\text{target}$ is swept in ascending order. A pair exits at the first $\varepsilon$ where the attack succeeds, defining a bracket $[\varepsilon_\text{lo}, \varepsilon_\text{hi}]$; pairs that resist even the $10\times$ sentinel are classified as resistant. Second, $N_\text{bisect} = 10$ binary search steps narrow the bracket to a refined $\varepsilon_\text{min} \approx \varepsilon_\text{hi}$.

The choice of $K = 20$ is confirmed sufficient by the ablation below: the fractional improvement in mean $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ beyond $K = 20$ is negligible. The output is a per-pair minimum budget, which serves as both a robustness measure and a candidate membership inference signal.

\paragraph{PGD iteration ablation.}
\label{ssec:ev_spectral_k}
To verify that $K = \kOriginal{}$ is a sufficient number of PGD steps, the grid sweep was re-run on \nSampleAblation{} sampled pairs at $K \in \kValues{}$. Mean $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ decreases as $K$ grows; the lowest value is reached at $K = \kBestEps{}$ (\kBestMeanEpsMin{} sensor units). The ratio $\varepsilon_\text{min}(K{=}\kBestEps{}) / \varepsilon_\text{min}(K{=}5)$ is \kConvergenceRatio{}: most of the gain comes in the first few doublings, and the incremental improvement between $K = \kOriginal{}$ and $K = \kBestEps{}$ is small relative to the total range. The K=20 setting is retained as it delivers the bulk of the available improvement at a fraction of the compute.

\subsection{Fixed-Budget Step-Count Search}
\label{sec:ev_nmin}
\label{ssec:ev_nmin_param}

The second parameterisation fixes the total L2 budget at $\varepsilon_\text{target}$ and instead varies the number of steps $N \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\}$. The per-step size is set uniformly to $\alpha = \varepsilon / N$ and applied to all $N$ steps: as $N$ increases each step becomes finer and the optimiser can navigate the loss landscape more precisely within the same ball. The minimum step count $N_\text{min}$ at which the attack first succeeds measures how much gradient resolution is required to breach the boundary, complementing the budget measure from the first parameterisation.

Two $\varepsilon$ conditions are tested: $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\text{target}$ and $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\text{target}/16$. The tighter budget forces finer gradient navigation and amplifies any member/non-member asymmetry that coarser steps would mask. Both the test split (non-members) and the training split (members) are attacked so the membership asymmetry can be observed directly from the ASR curves. The choice of $\varepsilon_\text{target}/16$ is motivated by the per-dataset $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ distributions: this value falls near their median, placing the attack in a regime where a meaningful fraction of pairs remain unsolvable. Chapter~\ref{ch:mia} analyses the budget sensitivity in detail.

The attack succeeds on the majority of pairs across all datasets and both parameterisations. In every case NN pairs have the highest ASR and lowest mean budget; MM pairs are the hardest, requiring the largest perturbation and the most steps. MN and NM fall between the two. This ordering is the first indication of a membership signal and is examined in Section~\ref{ssec:ev_asymmetry}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Membership Asymmetry}
\label{ssec:ev_asymmetry}

Both parameterisations converge on the same finding: enrolled subjects (MM) are systematically harder to attack than held-out subjects (NN). Under $\varepsilon_\text{min}$, the MM budget distribution is shifted right relative to NN (left panels of Figures~\ref{fig:combined_whugait}--\ref{fig:combined_wisdm}). Under $N_\text{min}$, this translates directly into lower cumulative ASR at every step count (right panels and Table~\ref{tab:combined_whugait}--\ref{tab:combined_wisdm}).

The asymmetry strength varies by dataset. On whuGAIT the gap in ASR is largest at $N = 1$ and narrows toward $N = 128$. On UCI-HAR the gap is smaller and emerges from $N = 4$. On WISDM it is near zero, consistent with WISDM's weaker $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ budget gap. The cross-pool types MN and NM fall between MM and NN in both ASR and mean budget, as expected for pairs where one subject is enrolled and one is not.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:ev_analysis}

\subsection{Feature-Space Analysis}
\label{ssec:ev_features}

The attack optimises the input end-to-end through both the CNN and the LSTM, but this does not indicate which component is being exploited. Figure~\ref{fig:feature_shift} shows the CNN embedding distance before and after perturbation on 200 sampled whuGAIT pairs. The scatter plot reveals that adversarial distances are nearly identical to clean distances (points lie on the diagonal) and the ratio histogram is sharply concentrated at 1.0 (mean ratio 1.006). The attack does not substantially close the CNN representation gap between the two subjects; the evidence is consistent with the perturbation acting on the LSTM's decision boundary rather than on the CNN embedding. A similar pattern is observed across the other dataset configurations.

\subsection{Spectral Analysis and Detection Resistance}
\label{ssec:ev_spectral}

The analysis in this subsection is performed on the adversarial examples produced by the $\varepsilon_\text{min}$ attack ($K = 20$ fixed steps, budget found via grid sweep and binary refinement). A spectral comparison between the two parameterisations is left for future work.

A perturbation small in $\ell_2$ could still be detectable if its energy concentrates at frequencies outside the biological range of human gait. Human gait produces most IMU signal energy below 10\,Hz (stride frequency and harmonics); above that band the signal decays toward the sensor noise floor.

Figure~\ref{fig:spectral_psd} shows the mean PSD for whuGAIT. In the gait band the perturbation (green dotted) sits well below both clean and adversarial signals, which are nearly indistinguishable. Above $\sim$20\,Hz, where the clean gait signal has already decayed to negligible absolute power, the perturbation decays more slowly and the adversarial signal locally exceeds the clean in the accelerometer channels; the gyroscope channels do not show this effect. This high-frequency excess is real but small in absolute terms: most perturbation energy falls in the 0--10\,Hz gait band, and a Mann-Whitney test on the fraction of total signal energy above 10\,Hz finds no significant difference between clean and adversarial groups.
