\section{Gait-Based Biometric Authentication}
\label{sec:rel_gait_auth}

\subsection{Early Approaches and Feature Engineering}
\label{ssec:rel_gait_early}

Interest in gait as a biometric signal dates to the early 2000s, when Gafurov et al.~\cite{gafurov2007gait} demonstrated that accelerometer recordings from waist-mounted devices could distinguish individuals at rates well above chance using handcrafted cycle-level features. These features proved brittle when device placement or walking speed varied. The UCI-HAR~\cite{anguita2013human} and WISDM~\cite{weiss2019smartphone} datasets were originally collected for activity recognition but became common benchmarks for gait-related tasks due to their multi-subject, naturalistic recordings.

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches}
\label{ssec:rel_gait_deep}

Zou et al.~\cite{zou2020deep} showed that a CNN encoder trained directly on raw sensor windows captures person-specific temporal patterns with sufficient discriminability for both identification and pairwise authentication, without requiring explicit step detection. They combine the CNN encoder with an LSTM authentication head trained on pairs of CNN-encoded windows, decoupling representation learning from pairwise comparison. This two-stage architecture is the basis of the system evaluated in this work.

\subsection{Privacy in Gait Systems}
\label{ssec:rel_gait_privacy}

Privacy analysis of trained gait models has received considerably less attention than recognition accuracy. Malekzadeh et al.~\cite{malekzadeh2018mobile} address gait anonymisation, a different threat model: the goal is to remove personal attributes from raw sensor recordings before they leave the device, rather than to characterise what a trained model reveals after deployment. Work in adjacent domains — activity recognition, ECG authentication, speaker verification — has begun to examine post-training privacy risks, primarily through membership inference on classification models that produce softmax outputs~\cite{shokri2017membership}. These attacks do not transfer directly to authentication pipelines, where the model scores pairs of windows rather than assigning class probabilities to individual inputs. Li et al.~\cite{li2022userlevel} is the closest structural parallel: they study user-level membership inference on metric embedding models, where the membership signal must be constructed from representations rather than confidence scores. Milani~\cite{milani2024gait} is the closest direct predecessor: it analyses identity inference vulnerability in a gait identification pipeline and establishes that such vulnerability exists in sensor-based biometric models. This work extends the analysis to a pairwise authentication pipeline, examines the origin of the signal within the architecture, and evaluates a mitigation grounded in the continual learning framework used to train the system. No prior work has examined these questions in a pairwise setting, where neither the softmax signal nor direct embedding access is available to the attacker.

\section{Membership Inference on Machine Learning Models}
\label{sec:rel_mia_biometric}

\subsection{Foundational Work}
\label{ssec:rel_mia_foundations}

Shokri et al.~\cite{shokri2017membership} formalised membership inference by training shadow models with known membership labels and using their outputs to build an attack classifier. Yeom et al.~\cite{yeom2018privacy} grounded the attack theoretically: the training-test loss gap upper-bounds the attack advantage, directly connecting memorisation to vulnerability. Salem et al.~\cite{salem2019ml} showed that a single shadow model and no knowledge of the target architecture are often sufficient, lowering the bar for practical attacks. Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2022membership} unified these ideas into a likelihood ratio framework (LiRA) that is statistically optimal at any false positive rate and substantially outperforms earlier methods, particularly on models with small generalisation gaps; the offline variant is the formulation adopted here and is described in detail in Section~\ref{ssec:bg_lira}.

\subsection{MIA on Biometric Models}
\label{ssec:rel_mia_biometric_detail}

Standard MIA pipelines operate on the softmax output of a classifier, using the per-class confidence vector as the membership signal. Authentication models produce no such output: the model scores pairs of windows rather than assigning class probabilities to individual inputs, so the standard attack surface does not exist. He and Zhang~\cite{he2021quantifying} studied this problem in the context of contrastive learning, where models similarly produce embeddings rather than class probabilities. They adapted existing MIA methods to operate on the representation space and found that contrastive models are less vulnerable to membership inference than supervised classifiers, attributing this to their reduced tendency to overfit individual training examples. This work faces the same structural obstacle and derives an alternative membership signal from the distribution of pairwise authentication scores, as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:mia}.

\section{Adversarial Attacks on Authentication}
\label{sec:rel_evasion}

\subsection{Adversarial Examples}
\label{ssec:rel_evasion_foundations}

Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing} identified that small input perturbations can reliably change a classifier's output. Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2015explaining} proposed FGSM as a single-step gradient attack; Madry et al.~\cite{madry2018towards} showed that iterative PGD with random restart finds stronger examples and serves as a principled robustness benchmark. The evasion attack in this work uses PGD adapted to the pairwise authentication setting: the goal is a perturbed gait window that crosses the model's acceptance threshold.

\subsection{Adversarial Attacks on Biometric Systems}
\label{ssec:rel_evasion_biometric}

Sharif et al.~\cite{sharif2016accessorize} demonstrated physical adversarial attacks on face recognition via printed eyeglass frames. Gait authentication presents a different attack surface: the signal is a sensor recording, so perturbations are applied digitally rather than physically. This work evaluates such digital attacks and assesses whether the required perturbation budget is consistent with natural within-subject gait variability.

\section{Continual Learning and Privacy}
\label{sec:rel_cl}

\subsection{Catastrophic Forgetting and Gradient Isolation}
\label{ssec:rel_cl_forgetting}

Sequential training of neural networks causes earlier task representations to be overwritten as the network adapts to new tasks, a phenomenon known as catastrophic interference~\cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}. Kirkpatrick et al.~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} proposed elastic weight consolidation (EWC) to mitigate this: parameters identified as important for earlier tasks are penalised from changing, using the Fisher information matrix as a proxy for importance. Architectural approaches partition the parameter space more directly, routing each task through a designated subset of the network so that earlier tasks are structurally immune to new training. The CDML mechanism~\cite{milani2024gait} follows this partition approach: a per-task binary code applied at the CNN feature maps before the identification head provides gradient isolation across sequential training rounds without requiring stored data from earlier tasks.

\subsection{Privacy Connections}
\label{ssec:rel_cl_privacy}

Gradient isolation, the core mechanism behind architectural CL approaches, has a natural reading in privacy terms. When each training task influences only a designated part of the computation, the degree to which one task's data can be recovered from parameters intended for another is structurally limited. This intuition underlies federated learning, where gradient isolation across clients reduces cross-client data leakage as a side effect of preserving autonomy. The connection between CL and privacy has not, however, been studied in the context of biometric authentication. This work examines whether a per-task feature-map code designed for gradient isolation can be repurposed as a privacy gate: by conditioning the authentication surface on a key that is held server-side and never distributed, it may be possible to prevent an attacker who holds the CNN weights from extracting a membership signal. The analysis is specific to the CDML design~\cite{milani2024gait} and to the pairwise authentication pipeline evaluated here.

\section{Positioning of This Work}
\label{sec:rel_positioning}

This work applies identity inference to a pairwise authentication model, where the standard softmax-based attack surface does not exist and the membership signal must be derived from the distribution of pair scores. A dedicated signal disentanglement experiment isolates the contribution of the CNN encoder from that of the LSTM authentication head by training the two components on non-overlapping subject populations. The evasion attack is evaluated as a robustness measure, and the asymmetry observed between members and non-members in the attack results motivates a secondary analysis: the minimum perturbation budget and the minimum step count are examined as candidate identity inference signals, connecting the evasion and inference evaluations.

The broader framing concerns a distributed deployment scenario in which the CNN encoder is trained sequentially across multiple operators, each holding their own subject population and their own private key. The key conditions the CNN feature maps during training, allowing each operator to train independently without sharing raw data or interfering with other operators' representations. This work evaluates whether the same mechanism that enables this distributed training also provides privacy protection, by conditioning the authentication surface on a key unknown to the attacker. The evaluation does not address streaming authentication settings or physical attack surfaces.
